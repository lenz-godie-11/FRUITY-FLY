16,000 neurons. 3.74 million connections. A rescue controller trained on my Mac.

I built a navigation controller using a selected subset of measured fruit-fly wiring from MaleCNS v1.0, then visualized one of its successful missions in Blender.

The task: search for a hidden beacon, collect it, and return to the departure point.

What is inside the model?

• 16,000 neurons: computational units corresponding to identified neurons in the dataset. This is a selected subnetwork of the connectome—the map of neural wiring.
• 3,736,859 directed connections: distinct source-to-target neuron pairs. A→B and B→A are separate connections.
• 27,441,238 synaptic contacts: the dataset's contact counts across those pairs, aggregated into connection weights. Multiple contacts can contribute to one connection.

The controller uses a recurrent rate model: each neuron has a continuous activity value. Learning adjusts gains on the measured connections while retaining their topology and assigned signs, alongside learned input encoders and action readouts.

Of the 1,534 input neurons, 100 hold a learned home encoding, 100 retain visited-place information, and 1,334 encode observations. These memory mechanisms are engineered. Signals pass through the measured graph to 772 descending-neuron readouts that produce four movement choices.

Training combined teacher demonstrations with DAgger: collecting states visited by the learner and adding teacher-labelled corrective examples. I ran 6,800 optimizer updates locally using PyTorch's Metal/MPS backend on an M4 Pro Mac with 24 GB RAM. The training loop took about 26.5 minutes, excluding initial demonstration generation.

The environment contains a 7×7 grid, 16 obstacles, and a 192-move budget. The controller knows the obstacle map and its current cell; the beacon is detectable only locally. After departure, the base location must be retained in memory. A legal-action mask prevents wall moves.

On 128 unseen missions:
• 96 collected the beacon: 75%.
• 39 also returned to base: 30.5%.

Finding the beacon is currently more reliable than completing the return journey.

The video shows a selected successful 42-move replay. Its route and neural activity come from the recorded controller. Flight, ruins, and camera movement are cinematic staging of a grid-navigation experiment.

The brain inset shows simulated activity at 15,619 measured neuron positions; 381 neurons lack coordinates. It visualizes this engineered model's activity, rather than biological recordings.
